Sensory Inclusion

Inside the Aaron and Samantha Judge Sensory Room at Madison Square Garden

📅 September 15, 2026
✎ Editorial Team
⌚ 3 min read

Samantha Judge’s most visible contribution to sensory inclusion is not an event or a campaign but a room. The Aaron and Samantha Judge Sensory Room opened at Madison Square Garden in 2024, giving guests with sensory processing needs a permanent place to step away from crowd noise and bright lights without leaving the venue.

For Samantha, a New York–based board member of the nonprofit KultureCity, the project turned sensory-inclusion advocacy into something physical. The room sits inside a venue that hosts professional basketball and hockey, major concerts, and other large events, where noise, lighting, and crowds can make attendance difficult for people with autism, sensory processing differences, and other invisible disabilities.

What the Room Contains

The room is built around controlled input. Lighting can be adjusted rather than fixed, calming visuals replace arena screens, and comfortable seating gives guests a place to sit outside the flow of foot traffic. Tactile elements offer something to hold or touch, and a bubble wall provides slow, predictable visual motion that can help visitors settle.

Each element addresses a specific problem. Arenas are designed to amplify sound and spectacle; the sensory room reverses that design for the guests who need it. Someone who becomes overwhelmed during a game or concert has a defined place to go, can recover at their own pace, and can return to the event when ready.

Why a Permanent Space Matters

Temporary accommodations come and go with individual events. A permanent room changes the baseline. Families planning a trip to Madison Square Garden can know in advance that a quiet space exists, which removes one of the main reasons some households have avoided live events altogether.

That approach mirrors the model KultureCity uses across its partner venues, where staff training, sensory bags, and designated quiet areas are treated as standard infrastructure rather than special requests. Samantha’s seat on the KultureCity board of directors connects the MSG room to that wider network of sensory-inclusive venues.

The Research Background Behind the Advocacy

Judge’s interest in how people experience physical environments did not start with the sensory room. Her graduate work in kinesiology at Fresno State focused on human movement and injury risk among Major League pitchers, and it was recognized as the university’s Outstanding Master’s Degree Project in 2017. That training in how the body responds to stress offers useful context for her practical, evidence-minded approach to accessibility.

The room’s name places her alongside her husband, New York Yankees captain Aaron Judge, but the space reflects a cause she has pursued directly through board service and fundraising runs. Readers can follow her work through Samantha Judge on Vimeo.

Conclusion

The Aaron and Samantha Judge Sensory Room occupies a small footprint inside a large building, but it changes who can comfortably attend events there. For Samantha Judge, it stands as the most concrete result of her sensory-inclusion work in New York: a permanent, practical space built for guests who need a quieter way to take part.
